A practical handbook

Practice change without chasing perfection

Tend is not a task list. It helps you connect small actions to stable cues, rehearse alternatives to unwanted patterns, and recover constructively when life gets in the way.

01

Cue → action

Grow one small behavior

Choose a stable cue

Use an event that already happens: finishing breakfast, arriving home, or closing your laptop.

Make it very small

Define a minimum version that remains realistic on a difficult day. Doing more is optional.

Repeat in context

When the cue appears, perform the action in the real world and record the repetition afterward.

Example: When I put my breakfast plate in the sink, I will walk outside. On a difficult day, standing outside for 30 seconds counts.

02

Trigger → replacement

Give an unwanted pattern another path

Map the pattern

Notice the recurring trigger and the need the current response serves—perhaps relief, stimulation, comfort, or connection.

  • Name the behavior without judging yourself.
  • Identify the context immediately before it.
  • Change the environment where practical.

Plan what you will do

A positive replacement is more actionable than a prohibition. Try to choose an alternative that addresses the same need.

  • “When X occurs, I will do Y.”
  • Avoid relying only on “I will not do X.”
  • Record each time you rehearse the alternative.

Example: When I feel tense and reach for my phone, I will take three breaths and walk to the window first.

03

Notice → learn → adapt

A lapse does not erase practice

Notice

Record that the opportunity was difficult or that the unwanted response occurred. No points are removed.

Learn

If useful, note what changed: fatigue, an unstable cue, an action that was too large, or environmental friction.

Adapt

Make the action smaller, adjust the cue, prepare the environment, and choose the next realistic opportunity.

Missing once is not a verdict. Habit formation varies greatly between people and behaviors. Tend intentionally avoids broken-streak warnings, health damage, and shame-based reminders.

04

Support, not replacement

How the garden grows

Real repetitions create growth

The garden changes when you record a healthy repetition or successfully use a replacement. There is no currency, shop, or endless task grind.

Reflection builds resilience

Useful lapse reflections are visible as recoveries. They acknowledge learning without rewarding the lapse itself.

Automaticity is self-reported

Occasionally rate how naturally the action follows its cue. The rating is a trend—not a diagnosis or competitive score.

You decide when to maintain

Move a stable habit into maintenance when it feels appropriate. Milestone labels are interface guidance, not scientific deadlines.

05

Research basis

What informed the design

Tend translates findings from habit and behavior-change research into product choices. Evidence differs in strength and does not guarantee an outcome. Gamification evidence is promising but limited, so the garden is intentionally modest and subordinate to real-world action.

  • Gardner, Lally & Wardle — Making health habitual
  • Lally et al. — Modelling habit formation in the real world
  • Wood & Rünger — Psychology of Habit
  • Adriaanse et al. — Breaking habits with implementation intentions
  • Adriaanse et al. — Ironic effects of negating unhealthy habits
  • Quinn et al. — Monitor those bad habits
  • Phillips & Hine — Self-compassion and health behaviour meta-analysis
  • Johnson et al. — Gamification for health and wellbeing review

Health boundary: Tend is not treatment. Behaviors involving addiction, eating disorders, self-harm, compulsions, or serious medical risk deserve support from an appropriately qualified professional.
